# Does one wet month mean a wetter climate?

**No. One unusually wet month cannot by itself establish that a local climate is getting wetter.** The Met Office describes climate over periods usually spanning 30 years ([S1](https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/climate/climate-explained/what-is-climate)). Our inference from this timescale distinction is that a single month's departure from a baseline does not demonstrate a long-term change; this is explanatory reasoning, not a local study result.

## A fictional example

Example Town and its September rainfall of **100 mm** are fictional inputs. Its same-month 1991–2020 baseline of **80 mm** is assumed for this exercise, not calculated from local observations.

- Difference: 100 − 80 = **20 mm above baseline**.
- Percentage above baseline: (100 − 80) ÷ 80 × 100 = **25% above baseline**.
- Percentage of baseline: 100 ÷ 80 × 100 = **125% of baseline**.

A monthly normal averages the same calendar month across years—for example, September across 1991–2020—not 30 consecutive months. NOAA describes official U.S. normals using a uniform 30-year period. We use that source only to explain the method, not to supply local or UK rainfall data ([S2](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/land-based-station/us-climate-normals)).

## Three different questions

An **anomaly** describes a departure from a baseline: here, September is 20 mm wetter than the assumed September normal. A **trend** describes change over time. Comparing one month with a long-term average does not show how rainfall has changed through the years; that distinction follows from the climate and normals explanations ([S1](https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/climate/climate-explained/what-is-climate), [S2](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/products/land-based-station/us-climate-normals)).

**Attribution** investigates causes and requires multiple lines of evidence ([S3](https://psl.noaa.gov/csi/why/)). Individual events can be investigated for a climate-change influence: event-attribution studies compare simulations with and without human influence, alongside observations and other inputs ([S4](https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/climate/understanding-climate/attributing-extreme-weather-to-climate-change)). No such study was performed for this fictional month.

## What further evidence is needed?

To investigate a local trend, we would need a long local rainfall time series covering many years, with comparable calendar months, seasons and annual totals as appropriate to the question. We would also need records of station location, instruments, measurement methods and missing observations to assess comparability, plus uncertainty in observations, the baseline and any estimated trend. These are proposed evidence requirements, not analyses completed here.

The supplied totals alone establish neither how rare this month would be nor whether it qualifies as extreme. Those questions need the distribution and variability of local rainfall. This brief provides no forecast, measured local trend or attribution result.

**Suggested newsletter wording:** “In our fictional example, September rainfall was 100 mm—20 mm, or 25%, above the assumed September baseline. One wet month does not establish a wetter climate; a reliable long-term local record and analysis are needed.”
